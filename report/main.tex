\title{\textbf{Accelerating ColBERT MaxSim with BOND-Style Dimension Pruning on a PDX Layout}}
\author{Martan van der Straaten\\\textit{Radboud University}}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
ColBERT scores documents with late interaction: every query token is compared
against every document token, which makes exact retrieval expensive. BOND
showed, for single-vector search on a vertically decomposed layout, that
scanning dimensions incrementally and pruning candidates with a partial-score
bound can skip most of this work. In this paper, we investigate whether that
idea transfers to ColBERT's MaxSim operator on the modern PDX layout, on a
single CPU node. We formalize the bound for MaxSim, prove it exact-safe, and
implement it in a fused vectorized kernel whose dense variant outperforms an
OpenBLAS baseline by roughly $3\times$. We find that the mechanism transfers
only partially: the Cauchy--Schwarz bound stays too loose to prune anything
until late in the dimension scan, so early pruning never pays for its
bookkeeping. Pruning at late checkpoints does discard 88--98\% of documents
exactly, but at that point most of the memory traffic has already been paid,
and the wall-clock win over the fused dense baseline is decisive on only one
of four datasets. Evaluated as a retrieval system, the exact-safe kernel
matches full-scan nDCG@10 at essentially no cost, while the fixed-budget
candidate-generation pipelines we test are slower at equal or lower quality
on all but the largest corpus.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Neural retrieval models rank documents by comparing dense vector
representations of queries and documents. ColBERT~\cite{khattab2020colbert}
takes a fine-grained approach: instead of one vector per document, it keeps
one vector per \emph{token}, and scores a query--document pair with late
interaction,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{score}(q, d) \;=\; \sum_{i} \max_{j} \,\langle q_i, d_j \rangle ,
  \label{eq:maxsim}
\end{equation}
the sum over query tokens of the best-matching document token. This MaxSim
operator is more accurate than single-vector retrieval, but it is also much
more expensive: a query with $m$ tokens against a corpus of $N$ token vectors
costs $m \times N$ inner products of dimension $D$. Production systems avoid
this cost with approximate indexes such as PLAID~\cite{santhanam2022plaid},
which trade exactness for speed. This paper studies the complementary
lever: reducing the scoring work while keeping the result exact.

BOND~\cite{devries2002bond} offers a candidate mechanism. On a vertically
decomposed (column-per-dimension) layout, it scans dimensions incrementally,
maintains a partial score per candidate, and discards a candidate as soon as
an upper bound on its final score falls below the current top-$k$ threshold.
The recent PDX layout~\cite{kuffo2025pdx} revives this vertical design for
modern single-instruction-multiple-data (SIMD) hardware and shows large
speedups for single-vector search. Both
results are for one query vector and one score per candidate. MaxSim breaks
both assumptions: there are $m \approx 21$ query tokens, and the score is a
sum of maxima over a variable-length set of document tokens. Whether
dimension pruning survives this change is not obvious; this transfer is the
subject of this paper.

\subsection{Research questions}

Our primary research question is:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Can a PDX-compatible implementation of BOND-style dimension pruning
accelerate ColBERT MaxSim retrieval at matched retrieval quality on a single
CPU node?}
\end{quote}

We decompose it into four subquestions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RQ1 (mechanism).} How much work can a valid MaxSim bound
    save in principle, and where in the dimension scan does pruning become
    possible?
  \item \textbf{RQ2 (baseline).} What is the strongest honest dense MaxSim
    baseline on this hardware, and does the PDX layout itself matter in the
    MaxSim regime?
  \item \textbf{RQ3 (exact-safe gate).} Does exact-safe BOND pruning beat
    that dense baseline in wall-clock time?
  \item \textbf{RQ4 (system).} How does the pruned kernel compose with
    candidate-generation methods, compared fairly at fixed candidate
    budgets?
\end{itemize}

We answer RQ1 with accounting instruments that measure bound tightness and
candidate survival independently of any implementation, RQ2 and RQ3 with a
purpose-built fused kernel measured against its own dense variant, and RQ4
with an integration study in which every method is timed end-to-end on the
same machine at the same candidate budget. The approximate regime, where the
bound is deliberately shrunk below its safe value, is out of scope for this
paper; we return to it in the discussion.

\subsection{Contributions}

The contributions of this paper are the following:

\begin{itemize}
  \item We formalize BOND-style dimension pruning for MaxSim and prove the
    resulting method exact-safe, at both the document and the token level,
    including its transfer to a checkpointed, panel-blocked kernel.
  \item We design a fused MaxSim kernel on the PDX layout that outperforms a
    multithreaded OpenBLAS dense baseline by roughly $3\times$. Beyond
    serving as the baseline for the pruning experiments, this kernel is a
    contribution in its own right, independent of pruning.
  \item We explain \emph{why} the BOND mechanism largely fails for MaxSim:
    the Cauchy--Schwarz envelope stays wider than ColBERT's score gaps until
    roughly 75\% of the dimensions are paid for. Late-checkpoint pruning is
    the one operating point where the mechanism fires.
  \item We contribute a measurement methodology, interleaved baseline timing
    and fixed candidate budgets, that isolates mechanism effects from
    measurement artifacts in wall-clock comparisons.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section~\ref{sec:background}
covers the background: late interaction, BOND, and the PDX layout.
Section~\ref{sec:bound} formalizes the bound and proves it exact-safe;
Section~\ref{sec:kernels} presents the kernels built on it.
Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the experimental setup and how each
experiment answers a research question. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents
the results, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses and concludes.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

In this section, we briefly cover the three research lines this paper builds
on, and the baseline systems we compare against.

\subsection{ColBERT and late interaction}

Dense retrieval encodes queries and documents as vectors and ranks by vector
similarity. Single-vector models compress each document into one embedding,
which is cheap to search but lossy. ColBERT~\cite{khattab2020colbert} instead
keeps one embedding per token and defers the interaction between query and
document to search time, through the MaxSim score of
Equation~\ref{eq:maxsim}: each query token picks the document token that
matches it best, and the document's score is the sum of these best matches.
All token embeddings are unit-normalized, so each inner product is a cosine
similarity. Late interaction retains much of the effectiveness of full
cross-attention rerankers while keeping document representations precomputable
\cite{santhanam2022colbertv2}. The price is scoring cost: the corpus holds an
order of magnitude more vectors ($N$ tokens rather than $N$ documents), and
every query token must visit all of them for an exact result.

\subsection{BOND: dimension-incremental pruning}

BOND~\cite{devries2002bond} accelerates exact nearest-neighbor search on a
vertically decomposed store: values are grouped by \emph{dimension}, not by
vector, so the engine can scan dimension 1 of every candidate, then dimension
2, and so on. After scanning a prefix of dimensions, every candidate has a
partial score, and a bound on what the unscanned remainder can still
contribute. A candidate whose optimistic bound cannot reach the current
$k$-th best score is discarded without reading its remaining values.
Correctness is immediate as long as the bound is a true upper bound, which
makes the method \emph{exact-safe}: it returns exactly the top-$k$ of a full
scan. The appeal is that the threshold rises as good candidates finish, so
later candidates are pruned earlier; the risk is that the bound is loose, in
which case nothing is pruned and the bookkeeping is pure overhead. Notably,
the original paper reports that for Euclidean search a \emph{cheap}
query-side bound beat their tighter bound at wall-clock, because the tighter
bound's bookkeeping did not pay off. Section~\ref{sec:kernels:bond} revisits
this trade-off for MaxSim, where the outcome is the opposite.

\subsection{The PDX layout}

PDX~\cite{kuffo2025pdx} is a recent data layout for vector similarity search
that decomposes vectors \emph{within blocks}: inside a block of vectors,
values are stored dimension-major, so a SIMD register can process one
dimension of many candidates at once. This gives distance kernels that
auto-vectorize cleanly, and it makes dimension-incremental pruners such as
BOND and ADSampling~\cite{gao2023adsampling} natural to express: pruning a
candidate means masking a lane, and stopping early means not loading the
remaining dimension columns at all. The published PDX results are, like BOND,
for single-vector queries ($m = 1$): each corpus value is loaded, used in one
multiply-add, and discarded, so the workload is memory-bound and layout plus
pruning are the only levers. An important observation for this paper is that
MaxSim changes this regime: with $m \approx 21$ query tokens, every loaded
document value is reused $m$ times. In linear-algebra terms, the workload
moves from a general matrix--vector multiplication (GEMV), which is
memory-bound for every implementation, toward a general matrix--matrix
multiplication (GEMM), where register blocking and operand reuse dominate
performance. A pruning mechanism
therefore has to beat a much stronger dense baseline than in the published
$m = 1$ setting.

\subsection{Candidate generation baselines}

The alternative to scanning the full corpus is generating a small candidate
set first. Inverted-file (IVF) indexes cluster the corpus and probe only the
nearest clusters at query time; we use the FAISS
implementation~\cite{douze2024faiss}
with an exact MaxSim rerank on top, aggregating per-token search hits into
document candidates. PLAID~\cite{santhanam2022plaid} is the ColBERT-native
pipeline, combining centroid-based candidate generation with compressed
scoring stages. Both are approximate: their recall depends on how many
candidates are probed. In our comparisons, all such methods are evaluated at
a \emph{fixed candidate budget}, so a candidate-generation speedup can never
masquerade as a scoring speedup (Section~\ref{sec:method:protocol}).

\section{The BOND-MaxSim bound}
\label{sec:bound}

\subsection{Why pruning needs a bound}
\label{sec:bound:why}

An exhaustive MaxSim scan pays $m \times N \times D$ multiply-adds per
query, although most documents end up nowhere near the top-$k$. BOND
instead scans dimensions incrementally and abandons a document as soon as
an upper bound on its final score, computable from the scanned prefix
alone, falls below the running $k$-th best score $\tau_k$. For Euclidean
search this bound is nearly free: a partial squared distance only grows, so
it already bounds the final distance, with no slack and at the cost of one
comparison. An inner product has no such monotonicity; its unscanned
dimensions can raise or lower it, so a partial MaxSim score proves nothing
by itself. What is needed, and what this section derives, is a valid bound
on the unscanned remainder.

\subsection{Deriving the bound}
\label{sec:bound:rules}

After scanning a prefix $S$ of the dimensions, the partial score
$P_{ij}(S) = \sum_{z \in S} q_{iz} d_{jz}$ of a token pair is known; the
remainder, the inner product of the two unscanned subvectors, is not. The
remainder is largest when those subvectors align perfectly, in which case
it equals the product of their lengths. This is the Cauchy--Schwarz
inequality:
\begin{equation}
  \big|\langle q_i^{S^c}, d_j^{S^c}\rangle\big| \;\le\;
  \|q_i^{S^c}\| \cdot \|d_j^{S^c}\| \;=:\; R_{ij}(S).
\end{equation}
Unit-norm tokens make these lengths free,
$\|x^{S^c}\|^2 = 1 - \sum_{z \in S} x_z^2$, so every pair carries a valid
interval $P_{ij} \pm R_{ij}$ at every prefix, at no extra memory traffic.
Assuming the best case for every token pair gives the document bound
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{score}(q, d) \;\le\; \sum_i \max_j \big( P_{ij}(S) + R_{ij}(S) \big)
  \;=\; \mathrm{UB}_d(S),
  \label{eq:ubd}
\end{equation}
and the BOND rule carries over: document $d$ is discarded as soon as
$\mathrm{UB}_d(S) < \tau_k$. The same intervals give a \emph{token-level}
test: writing $U_{ij} = P_{ij} + R_{ij}$ and $L_{ij} = P_{ij} - R_{ij}$, a
document token $j$ is dropped once
$U_{ij} < L_i := \max_{j' \in \mathrm{Live}} L_{ij'}$ for every query
token $i$.

\subsection{Correctness}
\label{sec:bound:safety}

\begin{theorem}[exact safety]
\label{thm:safety}
Under unit-norm token embeddings, exact arithmetic, and top-$k$ by set
equality, the pruned scan at $\mathrm{shrink} = 1$ returns the exact top-$k$
of Equation~\ref{eq:maxsim}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The key step is a \emph{survival invariant}: no query token's true best
match $j^*(i) = \arg\max_j \langle q_i, d_j\rangle$ is ever dropped. Fix
$i$ and suppose $j^*(i)$ is live. Every live token $j'$ satisfies
$L_{ij'} \le \langle q_i, d_{j'}\rangle \le \langle q_i, d_{j^*(i)}\rangle
\le U_{ij^*(i)}$; taking the maximum over $j'$ gives $L_i \le U_{ij^*(i)}$,
so the domination test never fires for $j^*(i)$, and by induction over
pruning rounds it stays live. Consequently, a document that survives to
full scan depth, where all residuals are zero, finalizes with
$\sum_i \max_{j \in \mathrm{Live}} P_{ij}$ equal to its exact score. The
threshold $\tau_k$ is therefore always the $k$-th best \emph{exact} score
among finalized documents, and a document discarded by
$\mathrm{UB}_d(S) < \tau_k$ satisfies
$\mathrm{score}(q,d) \le \mathrm{UB}_d(S) < \tau_k$, so it cannot belong to
the final top-$k$.
\end{proof}

Three remarks complete the picture. First, unit normalization is a
\emph{safety} precondition, not a convenience: a stored norm above~1 makes
the residual too small and breaks exactness silently, so the pipeline
verifies norms at export time. Second, the proof transfers to the blocked
kernel of Section~\ref{sec:kernels:bond}: a checkpointed, document-level
bound is looser than the per-dimension one and therefore still safe;
padding a document by duplicating one of its own tokens leaves every
$\max_j$ unchanged; and a threshold shared across threads only rises toward
the true $\tau_k$ from below. Third, the bound admits an approximate mode
that scales the residual by a factor $\mathrm{shrink} < 1$, trading recall
for pruning; no recall guarantee survives this, and everything in this
paper runs at $\mathrm{shrink} = 1$.

\section{Kernel designs}
\label{sec:kernels}

\subsection{A fused dense kernel}
\label{sec:kernels:dense}

A pruning claim is only meaningful against a strong dense baseline: a
pruned kernel that beats a weak scan demonstrates nothing about pruning.
For MaxSim, the strong dense formulation is a matrix product. Stack the $m$
query tokens as rows of $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times D}$ and all $N$ corpus
tokens as rows of $X \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times D}$; then $Q X^\top$ contains
every token-pair similarity, and Equation~\ref{eq:maxsim} follows by taking
the maximum per document and summing. Matrix multiplication is the most
heavily optimized routine in numerical computing, available through
multithreaded Basic Linear Algebra Subprograms (BLAS) libraries. Computed
this way, exact MaxSim runs nearly an order of magnitude faster on our
hardware than a straightforward columnar scan, confirming the GEMM-regime
argument of Section~\ref{sec:background}.

Even so, a general-purpose BLAS performs three steps MaxSim does not need:
it re-packs the static corpus into cache tiles on every call, it
materializes the full $m \times N$ score matrix although only one maximum
per document survives, and the reduction then re-reads that matrix from
memory.

Our kernel removes all three. The corpus is packed once, at build time,
into panel-major PDX blocks: panels of 16 document tokens stored
dimension-major, documents padded to a panel multiple by duplicating their
last token (exact by Section~\ref{sec:bound:safety}). The panel width
matches the SIMD width of the hardware: one AVX-512 instruction, the widest
SIMD extension on current x86 CPUs, processes a 512-bit vector of 16
single-precision floats, i.e.\ one dimension of one full panel. At query
time, a register tile accumulates all $m$ query tokens against one panel,
and the per-document maximum folds in registers as each document's last
panel completes; the score matrix is never written.

The result computes identical exact scores at roughly $3\times$ the
throughput of the 12-thread OpenBLAS formulation ($2\times$
single-threaded). Beyond serving as the baseline for every pruning
experiment, this is a contribution on its own: the PDX layout pays off for
MaxSim through packing amortization and a fused reduction, not through the
memory-bound argument of the $m = 1$ literature.

\subsection{Fused BOND kernels}
\label{sec:kernels:bond}

The pruned kernel keeps the identical microkernel and adds pruning at a
small set of \emph{checkpoints} $C \subseteq \{1,\dots,D\}$. Documents are
processed one at a time, depth-first across dimension segments; at each
checkpoint the bound $\mathrm{UB}_d$ of Equation~\ref{eq:ubd} is tested
against a shared, rising threshold $\tau$, and a surviving document
finalizes exactly as in the dense kernel. Since pruning is the only
difference between the two arms, any wall-clock gap is attributable to the
mechanism; both arms produce bit-identical scores for unpruned documents,
verified on every dataset.

Three variants are measured: a document-level arm; a token-level arm that
additionally applies the domination test of Section~\ref{sec:bound:rules}
with lane masking; and a \emph{cheap-bound} arm that relaxes the
document-side residual to 1, the query-only bound of the original BOND
paper. The checkpoint set and the threshold policy (self-seeded, externally
seeded, or oracle) are parameters throughout.

The cheap-bound arm deserves a note in advance. For Euclidean search, the
original BOND study concluded that the cheap bound wins at wall-clock: it
costs little pruning and its bookkeeping is far cheaper. For MaxSim we find
the opposite (Section~\ref{sec:results}): the cheap bound prunes nothing at
any checkpoint where pruning is possible at all. The tightness a Euclidean
partial distance provides for free must, for an inner product, be bought
with the full Cauchy--Schwarz residual.

Finally, the threshold can be \emph{seeded}: any lower bound on the true
$\tau_k$ is safe, and a higher starting threshold means earlier pruning.
The seeded arm scores a handful of IVF-probed candidates exactly before the
scan starts. One further arm gives up exactness entirely: the
\emph{partitioned fused scan} clusters the documents at build time, packs
each cluster in panel layout, and scans only the clusters nearest to the
query with the fused kernel. This arm can miss true top-$k$ documents
and is therefore reported strictly as a recall--latency frontier.

\subsection{Measurement instruments}
\label{sec:kernels:instruments}

Wall-clock alone cannot answer RQ1, because a kernel conflates what the
bound \emph{could} prune with what its schedule manages to capture. Three
instruments are therefore kept separate from the throughput kernels. First,
a wide-block
\emph{accounting} kernel evaluates both pruning tests at every dimension
fetch, breadth-first over a block of tokens, and counts exactly which cells
of the (token $\times$ dimension) grid are touched: this is the upper
envelope of what any schedule could save, the mechanism under a microscope.
Second, a NumPy \emph{checkpoint simulator} replays the fused kernel's
document-level policy at a fixed threshold and predicts its pruning and cell
counts; it is validated against the kernel's own counters. Third, the exact
NumPy oracle provides ground-truth scores, and every exact-safe run is gated
on exact top-$k$ agreement with it (ties at the $k$-th rank are accepted by
score). Accounting numbers (cells touched) and wall-clock numbers are never
mixed in what follows: the former answer RQ1, the latter RQ3.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:method:setup}

\paragraph{Data and embeddings.} We evaluate on four BEIR
corpora~\cite{thakur2021beir}: NFCorpus (3{,}633 documents, 0.84M token
vectors), SciFact (5{,}183 documents, 1.14M), ArguAna (8{,}674, 1.51M), and
SciDocs (25{,}657, 4.53M). Documents and queries are embedded once with
GTE-ModernColBERT-v1 through PyLate~\cite{chaffin2024pylate}: $D = 128$,
\texttt{float32}, unit-norm token vectors (verified at export, per
Section~\ref{sec:bound:safety}), and variable-length queries of
$m \approx 21$ tokens on average. Documents are embedded text-only (titles
dropped), which lowers absolute qrels numbers relative to published BEIR
setups; every arm shares the same embeddings, so comparisons between arms are
unaffected. These embeddings are fixed across every
experiment; how each experiment queries and scores them is described in
Section~\ref{sec:method:experiments}.

\paragraph{Hardware and software.} All measurements run on one machine: an
AMD Zen~5 laptop CPU (Ryzen AI 7 445, 6 cores / 12 threads, AVX-512, 1\,MB
L2 per core) under Fedora Linux. The kernels are C++20, compiled with
Clang~21 at \texttt{-O3 -march=native} with OpenMP, and called through
\texttt{ctypes} from Python~3.12. The dense external reference is NumPy~2.4
backed by scipy-openblas~0.3.31; the candidate-generation baselines use
faiss-cpu~1.14 and the PLAID implementation of PyLate~1.6; qrels metrics
use ranx~0.3. Every arm is measured both single-threaded and on all cores,
and thread counts are always reported with the number.

\subsection{Measurement protocol}
\label{sec:method:protocol}

Two measurement controls are essential to the validity of the wall-clock
comparisons.

\paragraph{Interleaved baselines.} On a laptop-class CPU, thermal and
frequency state drift between runs, so a baseline timed in a separate run is
not comparable to a candidate arm; margins of the size at stake here (single
percents to tens of percents) can be produced by the measurement alone. All
arms are therefore timed \emph{interleaved}: round-robin within one process,
five to eight repetitions per arm with the fastest kept, and the dense
baseline measured in the same loop as the arms it is compared against.

\paragraph{Fixed candidate budgets.} When comparing candidate-generation
methods (RQ4), every method is granted the same budget $B$ of documents
eligible for full scoring, with $B$ on the axis. A method may fill its
budget with better candidates and win on recall, but it cannot win the
latency comparison by silently scoring fewer documents. Complete pipelines
sit inside the timer, including per-token hit aggregation, and index build
cost is reported separately from query latency. Without these constraints,
comparisons between such pipelines are dominated by budget and
timer-boundary choices rather than by the methods themselves.

\paragraph{Separation of regimes.} Exact-safe and approximate results never
share a table: exact-safe arms must pass the exact-agreement gate, and
approximate arms live on recall--latency frontiers. Similarly, accounting
(cells touched) and wall-clock are produced by different instruments and
reported apart.

\subsection{Experiments}
\label{sec:method:experiments}

Nine experiments, E1--E9, span the four research questions;
Table~\ref{tab:experiments} maps each to its research question and
instrument, and Section~\ref{sec:results} refers to them by number. Unless
an experiment states otherwise, it runs on all four corpora over a fixed
50-query subsample (drawn once per corpus with seed 42) at retrieval depth
$k = 10$; exact-safe arms are gated on exact top-$k$ agreement with the
NumPy oracle, approximate arms report recall against the exact top-10, and
the BOND arms use the tight bound, natural dimension order, and
$\mathrm{shrink} = 1$. Three threshold policies recur: \emph{self-seeded}
starts the threshold at $-\infty$ and lets it rise as documents finalize;
\emph{seeded} initializes it by exactly scoring a small candidate set
(Section~\ref{sec:kernels:bond}); \emph{oracle} initializes it at the true
$k$-th score, the upper limit of what any seeding can achieve.

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,topsep=3pt,leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{E1, bound slack} (RQ1): the ratio of the document upper
    bound $\mathrm{UB}_d$ to the exact score on a 33-point grid of scan
    depths, aggregated over all query--document pairs of the subsample, for
    three dimension orders (natural, variance-descending, PCA).
  \item \textbf{E2, survival} (RQ1): the fraction of documents and tokens
    the pruning tests of Section~\ref{sec:bound:rules} keep alive at each
    depth, for every combination of the three threshold policies and three
    dimension orders, on the accounting kernel.
  \item \textbf{E3, kernel comparison} (RQ2, RQ3): the fused dense arm
    against NumPy/OpenBLAS, and the document- and token-level BOND arms at
    checkpoints $C = \{32, 64\}$, across the three dimension orders.
  \item \textbf{E4, checkpoint sets} (RQ3): the document-level arm over
    eight checkpoint sets, $\{32\}$, $\{48\}$, $\{64\}$, $\{96\}$,
    $\{112\}$, $\{32,64\}$, $\{64,112\}$, and $\{32,64,96,112\}$, under the
    oracle threshold; the best late sets are then re-timed interleaved
    against the dense arm on all four corpora.
  \item \textbf{E5, bound tightness} (RQ3): the tight bound against the
    cheap query-only bound, at the early control $\{32,64\}$ and the three
    best late sets of E4, under the oracle threshold.
  \item \textbf{E6, fixed budgets} (RQ4): complete pipelines at candidate
    budgets $B \in \{100, 500, 1000, 5000\}$ capped at corpus size: the
    exhaustive fused scan, IVF-seeded BOND at $C=\{112\}$, FAISS-IVF
    token search (${\sim}4\sqrt{T}$ lists for $T$ corpus tokens, rounded to
    a power of two; 32 probes) with per-document hit aggregation and exact
    MaxSim rerank of exactly $B$ candidates, the same construction on the
    PDX library's IVF index, and PLAID (4-bit residuals, 8 probes, $B$ full
    scores).
  \item \textbf{E7, threshold seeding} (RQ4): the $C=\{112\}$ arm under
    five threshold policies, from self-seeded through a cheap IVF seed (4
    probes and 32 hits per query token, 10 candidate documents scored
    exactly) and a strong one (16 probes, 128 hits, 100 documents) to
    oracle, with the pruning recovered and the seeding cost reported
    separately.
  \item \textbf{E8, partition frontier} (RQ4): the partitioned fused scan
    with $P \approx \sqrt{N_{\mathrm{docs}}}$ partitions (rounded to a
    power of two; spherical $k$-means over unit-normalized mean document
    vectors, 15 iterations), swept over
    $\mathrm{nprobe} \in \{1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, P\}$, with the dense and the
    BOND kernel each used as the partition scanner.
  \item \textbf{E9, retrieval quality}: each family's best arm scored as a
    retrieval system: the fused scan, exact-safe BOND ($C=\{112\}$,
    self-seeded), the partitioned scan at nprobe 16 and 32, FAISS-IVF
    and PLAID at $B \in \{100, 1000, 5000\}$, and a BM25 reference
    (\texttt{bm25s}, Lucene scoring, $k_1{=}1.5$, $b{=}0.75$, English
    stopwords, Snowball stemming) indexing the same text-only documents.
    BM25 shares nothing with the embedding stack; it is timed under the
    same rep protocol but standalone, since at its latency scale the
    interleaving control is moot.
\end{itemize}

E9 runs in its own evaluation regime. It scores each method over the full
evaluable test-query set of every corpus (300 queries on SciFact, 323 on
NFCorpus, 200 each on ArguAna and SciDocs) at retrieval depth $k = 100$,
evaluating at cutoff 10 with nDCG@10, recall@100 and MRR@10 against the BEIR
relevance judgments. These qrels metrics are computed with \texttt{ranx} and
cross-validated against the CoRECT
framework's~\cite{caspari2025corect} \texttt{pytrec\_eval} on every
run.\footnote{The two evaluators break score-ties differently, so each run is
handed to both as its \emph{rank order} rather than its MaxSim scores.
Separating ties by $s_i - i\varepsilon$ fails: \texttt{pytrec\_eval} holds
scores in 32-bit floats, whose ULP at MaxSim magnitudes ($\sim$4.5) exceeds a
usable $\varepsilon$, so the separation is truncated away and near-equal
documents are re-tied and broken by doc-id (disagreements up to
$5\times10^{-4}$ nDCG@10 on NFCorpus). Integer ranks are exact in
\texttt{float32}, so both evaluators score the identical ranking and agree to
rounding.} Where an approximate arm lands above the exact scan, the
difference is tested with a two-sided paired permutation test over per-query
nDCG@10 (20{,}000 sign flips per pair).

Every wall-clock measurement follows the interleaved protocol of
Section~\ref{sec:method:protocol}, and index and partition builds are timed
separately and excluded from query latency. Each experiment writes its
results (dataset, arm, thread count, timing, pruning statistics, quality
gate) to one shared JSON schema, and every figure and table in
Section~\ref{sec:results} is generated from those files.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We report the four research questions in order. Throughout, figures show
SciFact as the representative corpus except where noted; the accompanying text
gives the range across all four datasets, and per-dataset numbers are
collected in the tables. All wall-clock numbers follow the interleaved
protocol of Section~\ref{sec:method:protocol}.

\subsection{RQ1: the bound is loose until late in the scan}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{e01_bound_slack_scifact}
  \caption{Bound slack (E1), the ratio of the document upper bound
  $\mathrm{UB}_d$ to the exact score, versus scan depth on SciFact, for
  three dimension orders (mean and 10th--90th percentile band over
  query--document pairs). Even at half the dimensions the bound still
  overestimates scores by ${\sim}5\%$, far wider than typical
  inter-document score gaps.}
  \label{fig:e01}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{e02_pruning_rate_scifact}
  \caption{Survival curves (E2) on SciFact: fraction of documents (left) and
  tokens (right) still alive versus dimensions scanned, per threshold policy
  and dimension order. Nothing is prunable before dimension 64 of 128; most
  pruning becomes possible only past dimension 96.}
  \label{fig:e02}
\end{figure}

E1 and E2 (Figures~\ref{fig:e01} and~\ref{fig:e02}) answer RQ1: the bound is
valid everywhere but too loose to act on until late in the scan. In E1, at
the first scanned dimension the Cauchy--Schwarz upper bound already
overestimates the exact
score by 11--15\% (6\% on ArguAna, whose scores are lower), and even at half
the dimensions it still overestimates by 3--7\%, far wider than the gaps
between neighboring documents' scores that a threshold test must resolve.
E2 turns this slack into survival: under every threshold
policy (self-seeded, seeded, oracle) and every dimension order (natural,
variance-descending, PCA), 98--100\% of documents and essentially all document
tokens are still live at dimension~64 of~128, and the median document does not
become prunable until the final dimensions. The accounting kernel makes the
implied ceiling precise: even counting every cell the pruning tests would ever
touch, an exact-safe schedule can avoid only 8--16\% of the
token$\times$dimension grid; the remaining 84--92\% of cells must be read
under any schedule. This is the ceiling every kernel in the next subsection
operates under.

\paragraph{The dense kernel.} Table~\ref{tab:dense} reports E3's dense arms
and answers RQ2. The
fused PDX kernel computes exactly the same scores as the OpenBLAS formulation
but is $1.8$--$2.7\times$ faster on one thread and $2.1$--$3.3\times$ faster on
all cores, roughly $3\times$ at the median. The win comes from two
inefficiencies a general BLAS cannot avoid on this workload, neither of which
the fused kernel pays: it re-packs the static corpus on every call, and it
materializes then re-reads the full $m\times N$ score matrix. Both dense
formulations dwarf a columnar $m=1$-style scan, confirming the GEMM-regime
argument of Section~\ref{sec:background}; the layout matters for MaxSim through
packing amortization and the fused reduction, not through the memory-bound
mechanism of the single-vector literature.

\paragraph{Exact-safe pruning.} Against this baseline the exact-safe arm can
save little, exactly as RQ1 predicts. In E3 and E4, at early checkpoints
such as $C=\{32,64\}$ the bound prunes under 2\% of documents on every
dataset, so the pruned arm only carries the bookkeeping and loses to dense
(Figure~\ref{fig:e08}). Pruning becomes real only at late checkpoints:
$C=\{96\}$ or $\{112\}$ discards 88--98\% of documents \emph{exact-safe}
(recall $1.0$, verified against the oracle), but by dimension~96--112 most of
the memory traffic is already spent. The tight bound is essential here: in
E5, the cheap query-only bound of the original BOND paper
prunes 0\% at every late checkpoint. This reverses the Euclidean finding: an
inner product's tightness must be bought with the full Cauchy--Schwarz
residual, not inherited for free. Table~\ref{tab:exactsafe} gives the
wall-clock margins of E4's interleaved re-timing over the dense baseline on
all cores. Only ArguAna clears a decisive margin ($+8.7\%$); SciFact,
SciDocs, and NFCorpus land within $+0.9$--$2.3\%$, inside the run-to-run
noise of a laptop CPU. The answer to RQ3 is therefore negative at the
interleaved all-cores baseline: the win is real on one dataset of four, too
narrow a base for a general claim.

\subsection{RQ4: candidate generation rarely pays off at this scale}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{e01_fixed_candidate_arms_scidocs}
  \caption{Fixed-budget comparison (E6) on SciDocs (25{,}657 documents, all
  cores).
  Each candidate-generation pipeline is timed end-to-end at candidate budget
  $B$, with the exhaustive fused scan (dashed) as reference. On this, the
  largest corpus, IVF pipelines cross below the exhaustive scan at small
  budgets; on the three smaller corpora they do not.}
  \label{fig:s01}
\end{figure}

At the corpus scales in this study, the exhaustive fused scan is the strongest
system in its own right. In the fixed-budget comparison (E6), every
token-level candidate pipeline (FAISS-IVF with exact rerank, the IVF index of
the PDX library with the same rerank, and PLAID) is slower than that scan at
matched budget and quality on NFCorpus, SciFact, and ArguAna, once per-token
hit aggregation is placed inside the timer. SciDocs is the exception
(Figure~\ref{fig:s01}): on 25.7k documents the
IVF pipelines cross below the exhaustive scan at $B=100$ (FAISS $28.6$~ms and
PDX $24.9$~ms against $65.5$~ms for dense, at recall $0.92$). We expect this
crossover to widen on larger corpora. Much larger PDX-over-PLAID factors have
been reported elsewhere; under our protocol these turn out to be artifacts of
mismatched budgets, timer boundaries, and software stacks, all of which the
fixed-budget control removes.

Two further results frame the exact-safe arm. First, in E7 a realistic
IVF-seeded
threshold recovers up to ${\sim}99\%$ of the oracle threshold's pruning, so
the margins of Table~\ref{tab:exactsafe} are in principle reachable without
an oracle. However, the seed itself costs $7$--$23$~ms per query, more than
the $1$--$4$~ms the extra pruning saves, so the free self-seeded policy
remains the better production choice. Second, in E8 the partitioned fused
scan traces a genuine \emph{approximate} frontier ($4\times$ at recall $0.92$ on
SciDocs, $5.2\times$ at $0.87$ on ArguAna). Its full-probe exact control is
$2$--$5\times$ slower than the monolithic scan, however, and its exact-safe
partition bound prunes no partitions: the bound-tightness problem of RQ3
recurs one level up.

\subsection{Retrieval quality}

The comparison so far judges each arm by exact agreement, recall against the
oracle, and wall-clock. E9 adds the axis those
leave out: quality on the BEIR relevance judgments, scored over the full
test-query set as described in Section~\ref{sec:method:experiments}.
Table~\ref{tab:ir} reports the
nDCG@10, recall@100, and MRR@10 of each family's best arm on all four
corpora, alongside its recall
against the exact top-10 and interleaved latency. Exactness carries no
quality cost and, here, no latency cost: the exact-safe BOND arm reproduces the
full-scan nDCG@10, recall@100, and MRR@10 to rounding at essentially the same
wall-clock as the dense scan, so the RQ3 negative result costs the retrieval
system nothing. On the three smaller corpora, no fixed-budget candidate
pipeline improves on the monolithic scan: FAISS-IVF reaches full-scan quality
only at $B{=}1000$, where it is $2$--$4\times$ slower than the dense scan, and
PLAID's compressed scoring stays short on recall@100 at every budget. On
SciDocs, the crossover of E6 carries into the quality table: FAISS-IVF at
$B{=}100$ halves the scan's latency at full-scan nDCG@10, though it remains
2.5 recall@100 points short. The partitioned fused scan is the fastest arm on
every corpus at nprobe~16, giving up between six nDCG@10 points on NFCorpus
and nothing on ArguAna, an operating point a latency-bound deployment might
accept where the IVF pipelines cannot.

The BM25 row anchors the absolute numbers. ArguAna and SciDocs are hard for
every system: BM25 lands at 0.306 and 0.130 nDCG@10 there, close to its
published BEIR values, so the low scores on those corpora reflect the
benchmarks rather than the pipeline. The MaxSim arms beat BM25 on all four
corpora, although by smaller margins than published late-interaction results,
which we attribute to the text-only encoding of
Section~\ref{sec:method:setup}; on ArguAna, BM25 even exceeds every MaxSim
arm's recall@100 (0.930 against 0.885) while ordering its top-10 worse.
However, BM25 answers a query in 0.1--0.5~ms, $60$--$150\times$ faster than
the exhaustive scan. On corpora of this size, late interaction buys its
quality margin at a large latency multiple, and that multiple is the cost
the kernels of this paper reduce.

Table~\ref{tab:ir} also contains an apparent contradiction: some approximate
arms score marginally \emph{above} the exact scan (the partitioned arms on
ArguAna; partitioned at nprobe~32 and FAISS at $B{=}100$ on SciDocs). How can
an approximate system beat an exact one? Exactness is defined with respect to
the MaxSim score, not to the relevance judgments. When partition probing
misses a document near rank 10, its replacement carries a marginally lower
MaxSim score, and at that depth the score barely correlates with relevance,
so the swap helps about as often as it hurts. The paired permutation test of
Section~\ref{sec:method:experiments} confirms this: none of the inversions is
significant ($p \ge 0.13$; at nprobe~32 on SciDocs only 10 of 200 queries
change at all). The genuine quality losses, by contrast, are: partitioned at
nprobe~16 loses significantly on SciFact and NFCorpus ($p < 0.001$), as does
FAISS at $B{=}100$ on NFCorpus and ArguAna ($p \le 0.02$). The test therefore
does separate real differences from noise at these query counts, and the
inversions fall on the noise side. Thus the qrels metrics saturate well before recall against the
exact top-10 does, and the latter remains the fidelity metric every
exact-safe claim in this paper is gated on.

\section{Discussion and conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Taken together, the four research questions give a clear and mostly negative
answer to the paper's central question. A valid MaxSim bound does exist and is
exact-safe (RQ1), but the Cauchy--Schwarz envelope it rests on is loose enough
that 84--92\% of the token$\times$dimension grid must be read before any
pruning is possible, which caps the mechanism at 8--16\% of the work. The
strongest honest baseline against which to measure that mechanism (RQ2) is not
a library GEMM but a fused PDX kernel, roughly $3\times$ faster because MaxSim
is GEMM-shaped and the layout amortizes packing and fuses the reduction. Set
against that baseline, exact-safe pruning wins decisively on only one of four
datasets (RQ3): late checkpoints prune 88--98\% of documents exactly, but by
the depth at which they fire most of the memory traffic is already spent.
Finally, at these corpus scales the exhaustive fused scan is itself the
strongest system (RQ4), beating every fixed-budget candidate pipeline except on
the largest corpus, and exact-safety adds no retrieval-quality or latency
penalty.

The RQ3 result is a negative result of a specific and useful kind: the
mechanism fails in the bound \emph{mathematics}, not in the engineering. The
result is not an implementation artifact: both arms share a bit-identical
microkernel, and halving the pruned arm's bookkeeping overhead did not change
the outcome. An inner product simply lacks the monotonicity that makes a
partial Euclidean distance a free, tight bound. Thus the tightness must be
bought with the full Cauchy--Schwarz residual, which does not become tight
until three-quarters of the dimensions are paid for. This also reverses
the original BOND finding that a cheap query-only bound wins at wall-clock.
For MaxSim, the cheap bound prunes nothing.

Two constructive contributions stand independent of the negative result. The
first is the fused dense MaxSim kernel, a $3\times$ exact speedup over a tuned
BLAS in its own right. The second is the measurement methodology of
interleaved baselines and fixed candidate budgets, without which several of our
own early numbers were inflated by the measurement rather than the method.

\paragraph{Limitations and future work.} Our conclusions are scoped to four
BEIR corpora up to ${\sim}26$k documents, one embedding model, and one
laptop-class CPU; the SciDocs crossover in RQ4 is a direct signal that the
candidate-pipeline balance shifts with scale, and confirming the exact-safe and
partitioned arms on corpora one to two orders larger is the clearest next step.
The paper deliberately stays at $\mathrm{shrink}=1$: the approximate regime,
where the residual is shrunk below its safe value to trade recall for pruning,
is where a loose bound is least penalized and is the natural place to look for a
positive result. Finally, the fused kernel leaves int8/VNNI throughput
unexploited, an orthogonal lever to pruning.

We conclude that BOND-style dimension pruning transfers to MaxSim in theory
and in exactness, but not, at these scales, in wall-clock. The lasting
contribution of this investigation is the dense kernel we built to test the
claim.
