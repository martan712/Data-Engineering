\section{IVF Candidate-Generation Results}
\label{sec:results-ivf}

This section evaluates the approximate two-stage architecture of
\cref{sec:three-stages}: IVF token retrieval, document selection at budget
$C$, and exact float32 MaxSim reranking. Results are approximate by
construction and are evaluated against the exact ranking; they are reported
separately from the exact-safe BOND results of \cref{sec:results-bond}.

\Cref{tab:scifact-ivf} presents the held-out SciFact comparison and
\cref{fig:quality-latency} plots the resulting quality--latency frontier.
Four observations follow directly from the artifacts.

\paragraph{Candidate pools are complete; residual loss is selector loss.}
For every one of the 40 held-out queries, the retrieved candidate pool (mean
619.3 documents) contains the entire exact top-10. Loss at finite $C$ is
therefore attributable to the zero-filled approximate selector, not to token
retrieval. Reranking $C = 200$ documents per query recovers 0.975 of the exact
top-10 while evaluating 3.86\% of all query--document pairs; reranking the
complete pool recovers the exact ranking with 11.95\% of the pairs.

\paragraph{FAISS-IVF and PDX-IVF are quality-identical.} The artifacts record
that candidate pools, selected candidates, retrieved-hit counts, and final
top-$k$ rankings are equal between the engines at every budget; consequently
their recovery curves in \cref{fig:quality-latency} coincide exactly.

\paragraph{No consistent engine latency advantage.} The latency winner changes
with the budget (PDX is faster at $C = 50$ and full pool; FAISS is faster at
$C = 100$, 200, and 400), and token-search stage medians differ by at most
about \SI{0.02}{\second} across the SciFact arms. The evidence therefore does
not support a PDX-specific retrieval advantage in this workload.

\paragraph{Qrels metrics cannot resolve the architecture differences.} Qrels
Recall@10 (0.985) and MRR@10 (0.902) are \emph{identical} for every arm in
\cref{tab:scifact-ivf}, even though exact-ranking recovery spans 0.86 to 1.00.
With incomplete qrels and 40 queries, exact-ranking recovery is the sensitive
metric; qrels metrics are reported for completeness only.

\subsection{NFCorpus transfer}
\label{sec:ivf-nfcorpus}

The identical stack, online boundary, and configuration ($L = 100$,
$\texttt{nprobe} = 8$, frozen selector) were applied to NFCorpus
(3{,}633 documents; 864{,}703 token vectors; 40 held-out queries).
\Cref{tab:nfcorpus-ivf} reports the results and the left panel of
\cref{fig:transfer} compares the recovery curves of both datasets.

The transfer is less forgiving than SciFact in one specific way: reranking the
\emph{entire} candidate pool reaches only 0.975 exact recall@10, and the pool
contains the complete exact top-10 for 37 of the 40 queries. The residual
0.025 loss is therefore \emph{candidate-pool coverage} loss---no selector
operating on this pool can repair it---whereas on SciFact all loss at finite
$C$ was selector loss. Candidate pools and final rankings again agree between
FAISS and PDX at every budget. The PDX token-search stage is slightly slower
than FAISS in every matched NFCorpus arm (e.g., median \SI{0.145}{\second}
versus \SI{0.128}{\second} at $C = 50$), but total online latency is nearly
tied at $C = 200$ and at the full pool, so the earlier conclusion stands: the
evidence shows no consistent PDX-specific advantage. Qrels Recall@10 moves
only from 0.2150 ($C{=}50$) to 0.2191 (full pool, equal to the exact
reference), confirming again that qrels cannot resolve these differences.
Offline index construction took \SI{12.27}{\second} (FAISS) and
\SI{14.67}{\second} (PDX).
